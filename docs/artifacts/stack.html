01 · The stack

Four systems, and how data moves between them

Nothing here is new except ReactMap. The pieces around it already exist and already work; 2.0 changes how ReactMap talks to them.

A scanner walks the world and produces raw game data. Golbat decodes it, keeps the live picture in memory, and answers questions about it. ReactMap asks those questions on your behalf and paints the answers. PoracleNG sends you a message when something you care about appears.

The one thing to notice

Golbat can push, but only in a limited way: it posts decode events to a fixed list of URLs in its config file, routed by named region rather than by what anyone is looking at, and a delivery that fails is simply gone. There is no websocket, no event stream, and no way to say "tell me about this box on the map". So live viewports come from asking repeatedly, and that shapes section 03.

The push is still worth taking where it fits. Gyms and stops change rarely and never expire, so ReactMap will subscribe to Golbat's fort events and keep only a slow safety-net poll for them, while Pokémon, high-volume and filtered hard on Golbat's side, stay on the fast poll.

02 · Where data comes from

Golbat holds the live world

Golbat keeps active Pokémon in memory, indexed spatially, and forts too once the operator turns that on. It answers bounding-box queries with server-side filtering. ReactMap 2.0 leans on that far harder than 1.x did.

The important shift: 1.x could talk to Golbat or fall back to reading scanner database tables directly, which meant every filter had to be written twice. 2.0 requires Golbat and deletes the second implementation.

Three things that constrain the design

Golbat cannot be asked "what changed". There is no incremental query, confirmed by searching the whole codebase. Every poll returns the full matching set and ReactMap works out the difference itself. We have proposed adding one upstream.

The fort scans are off by default, and marked as drafts. fort_in_memory is experimental and gates the eight scan and availability endpoints, which answer 503 when it is off; looking a single gym or stop up by id works either way. Upstream also stamps all eight with a Draft badge, meaning their shapes may still change. 2.0 accepts both risks knowingly.

Results are capped at 3,000 Pokémon and 9,000 forts per query by default, and the cap cannot be raised from the client side. When a response comes back truncated it says so, and ReactMap responds by splitting the viewport into quarters and asking again, never treating a truncated answer as complete.

03 · Getting it to you

One socket, and only what changed

Today every browser polls on a timer and receives a full viewport each time, most of it identical to the last one. 2.0 opens one connection and sends differences.

What the server remembers

For each open connection ReactMap keeps a map of entity id to a change stamp. Not the entities themselves: it already has those in hand when it builds a message, so storing them twice buys nothing.

Pokémon expire on their own, when the time is trusted

A Pokémon whose despawn time has been confirmed is removed by the browser on its own clock, with no message from the server. When Golbat has not confirmed the time it sends a guess instead, and it says which is which, so the browser only self-evicts on confirmed times and lets the server decide the rest. Removals are otherwise only needed when something leaves your viewport, stops matching your rules, or a raid ends.

The biggest saving is not the socket

It is a file. ReactMap currently ships the Pokémon master data to every visitor inside a GraphQL response, which means the browser cannot cache it and re-downloads it on a timer.

04 · Building rules

A rule is one sentence

The filter system is the part of ReactMap people find hardest, and it is the part 2.0 changes most. The design goal was that you should be able to read your own filters back.

Each rule names one thing and says what should happen when it appears. Leaving a field blank means "any". You never build a rule out of nested conditions.

Rules that differ only in which species they name are shown as one row. Editing the group edits all of them; editing one separates it out. Nothing about the grouping is stored, so it can always be undone.

When two rules both match

The coloured ring beside each rule in the list is its glow, the colour it paints around a matching Pokémon on the map. When several rules match the same Pokémon, its ring splits into segments, one per rule, so every colour you asked for survives.

Both rules apply, and there is no ordering to reason about. Each display attribute has its own rule for combining, chosen so that nothing a person asked for is silently discarded.

Combining matched rules

AttributeCombines byReasoning
Sizelargest winsyou asked for attention twice; give the louder one

Why one row per thing

A rule that named twenty species would have to answer questions like "which of these does the IV range apply to". Naming one thing per row deletes that question instead of answering it, and it happens to be exactly the shape Poracle stores tracking in, which makes the two systems line up without translation.

05 · Alerts

Poracle, on its own page

Notifications move out of the map's filter panel and become their own thing, with one provider instead of the current multi-provider arrangement.

A ReactMap rule and a Poracle tracking row are close cousins rather than twins, so the alerts page translates between them instead of forwarding blindly. The differences are small but real, and they are listed here because pretending they do not exist is how imports lose data.

Correction: this is a mapping, not a copy

An earlier draft of our design said a ReactMap rule and a Poracle rule were the same shape, so sending one across was a rename. Reading Poracle's source disproved that, and it is the largest error validation caught.

Poracle Pokémon rules have no identity key. A rule is its whole set of fields, and holding several rules for the same species is the intended design, not a mistake. So changing one field edits a rule in place, but changing two adds a second one.

League ranks live one per rule. A rule can carry an IV floor beside its one league, and both then have to hold at once, but a second league needs a second rule. So one ReactMap rule asking for hundos or a good Great rank or a good Ultra rank becomes three rules on Poracle's side, and the OR between them exists only because they are separate rows.

None of this is a blocker. It means the alerts page has to translate rather than forward, and has to re-read after writing, because the id returned when you create a rule is not usable.

How you are identified

Poracle does not have accounts. It has delivery destinations, keyed by your Discord or Telegram id, because that is where the message has to go. ReactMap signs its requests with one shared server secret, so ReactMap itself is responsible for making sure you can only reach your own alerts.

06 · Running it

What changes for operators

If you run a ReactMap today, this is the part that affects you. Collected in one place because these are the first questions that matter.

What becomes required

Golbat becomes mandatory. The fallback where ReactMap reads scanner database tables directly is deleted for Pokémon, gyms, stops and stations, so those four come from Golbat or not at all. Nests, routes and the other quiet layers keep their database path, because Golbat has no endpoint for them.

Two Golbat settings matter: fort_in_memory = true, without which gyms, stops and stations do not load, and a [[webhooks]] entry pointing at ReactMap if you want fort changes pushed rather than polled. Both are one config line.

What your users will notice

Logins carry over: existing passwords keep working, migrated by a script you run once, which refuses loudly and changes nothing if it finds accounts it cannot safely migrate, naming the exact rows so you can fix them first. Filters do not carry over, because 1.x kept them in each browser rather than on the server; everyone starts from the new rules system. Notifications move to their own page and to a single Poracle.

Rollback

2.0 runs beside your production map at its own address until you choose to switch, and it writes to its own tables. Your existing user table is read, never modified, so going back is turning 2.0 off.

08 · Open questions

What we are actually asking

This document exists to be disagreed with. These are the decisions that are still live, and the ones where being wrong would be expensive.

Live decisions

each of these could still go the other way
QuestionWhere it stands
Should rules have an order? Two of our own documents disagree. One says drag to reorder and first match wins; the other says no order, and each display property resolves on its own. The second is newer and argues the first discards intent a person expressed, but the first is what answers "why is this one big".
Is requiring an experimental Golbat flag acceptable? fort_in_memory is off by default. Without it there are no gym, stop or station scans at all. 2.0 currently assumes operators will turn it on.
Do we wait for an incremental query upstream? Golbat cannot say what changed, so ReactMap polls and diffs. We have proposed adding it. The design works without it and gets cheaper if it lands.
Should Mega raids and showcase contests be filterable? Both upstreams support them and our rules schema does not. Adding columns later is expensive, so this is close to a now-or-never call.
Does the text filter syntax come back? Currently deferred, to avoid shipping two ways of expressing the same thing. Power users rely on it today.
How much does a Poracle import promise? Poracle carries per-rule settings ReactMap has no column for, including costume, weight, time of day and per-rule area overrides. An import that silently drops them is data loss rather than a missing feature.

Byte counts and endpoint behaviour on this page were read out of source or measured on disk. Where a figure is a scenario rather than a measurement, the table says so. Where something could not be confirmed, it says so.
